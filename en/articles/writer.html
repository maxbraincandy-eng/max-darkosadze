The Written Word

The books of Max Darkosadze, and why a surgeon writes

24 August 20262 min read

Contents

Max Darkosadze started writing for an ordinary reason: the same questions kept being asked in corridors, and the answers were too long for a corridor.

Mr. Max's first book grew out of the notes he kept for the doctors training beside him. The next was written for patients and their families — an attempt to explain, in ordinary Georgian, what is actually happening in a hospital, and why the person in the white coat is sometimes unable to promise anything.

What he writes about

The books and the long pieces ask one question in three languages: how does a person decide when the full answer is not available? In medicine it is called risk, in aviation procedure, in philosophy will.

The text rarely offers advice and never a diagnosis. It shows what the work looks like from the inside, and leaves the question with the reader.

The books have found readers well outside the professions they describe, which their author maintains says more about the appetite of the reading public than about him.

A style learned at the table

Critics have noted that his prose reads like his operating: nothing decorative, nothing wasted, and a refusal to hurry the difficult passage. He edits heavily, and cuts most of all wherever he catches himself sounding impressive.

If a sentence needs the reader to admire the writer, it is doing the wrong job. Clarity is the only respect a writer can offer.

Max Darkosadze

Writing about the living

Writing from a medical life raises an obvious problem, and he treats it as the central one. Every clinical passage in his books is either published with explicit consent, or altered until the patient could not recognise themselves, or composed from several cases at once. Where he could not do this honestly, the story is not in the book.

The philosophical books that followed are treated separately on this site — they belong to a different argument, and they are the reason a surgeon is now read by people who will never set foot in a hospital.

Why a surgeon writes

The deeper argument runs through everything he publishes. Specialised knowledge that stays inside the speciality becomes a kind of private property, and private property invites suspicion. Where medicine is not explained, rumour explains it instead — and rumour, in a health system, costs lives.

The royalties from his books go to the foundation. He has said in interviews that this is the least interesting fact about them, and that he mentions it only because readers ask.
